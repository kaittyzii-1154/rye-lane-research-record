READING & THINKING

PASSAGES THAT HELPED ME RECONSIDER THE PROJECT

Reading the street
differently.

My initial question concerned attention: how could shopfronts encourage people to pause and notice everyday life? The readings below helped me reconsider what noticing could tell me, and what it could not. Each connection starts with a particular passage and follows the question it opened for the project.

Not everyone sees the same street.

Margarethe Kusenbach (2003), Street phenomenology: The go-along as ethnographic research tool, pp. 467–469. The passages about Ross’s attention to street lighting and Ross and Jill’s contrasting descriptions of the same garden.

Kusenbach describes how Ross, who had worked in street lighting, notices the height and effectiveness of lamps that other people pass without mentioning. She then records two residents describing the same garden as a jungle: one sees neglect, while the other appreciates the planting and sees care. The visible setting is shared, but the experience and judgement differ.

This made me reconsider the assumption behind asking people to “look again”. Someone who does not notice the detail I have selected may be attending to something else through their work, route or familiarity. Equally, my impression of a shopfront as cluttered is not a sufficient account of how it is used or maintained.

For Rye Lane, the question became more specific: what matters to the person looking, and what experience informs their interpretation? A trolley or a stack of boxes needed to be considered alongside the accounts of people using that space, rather than given a meaning through my drawing alone.

Trolleys and the context of everyday work

01 / 03

A small encounter needs conditions that allow it to happen.

Sophie Watson (2009), The magic of the marketplace: Sociality in a neglected public space, p. 1582. The paragraphs describing greetings and conversations near stalls, followed by the discussion of layout, food, drink and informal seating.

Watson moves from brief recognition and conversation to the physical conditions that make these encounters possible. In the markets she studied, places to eat, drink and sit — including informal seating — contributed to opportunities for social interaction.

I took from this a way to connect social life to spatial arrangements. Instead of treating a lively scene as evidence of community by itself, I could ask what gave people a reason or an opportunity to remain there. This also helped me think about the café account in my own material, where buying coffee sometimes became a conversation.

The connection is a question to investigate, rather than proof that an object on Rye Lane has the same use as one in Watson’s study. I cannot identify a box as seating simply because it appears in a photograph. I need to know how it is used and whether people actually stop there.

Street-side stalls and display structures

01 / 04

Who decides what an acceptable display looks like?

Sara González and Paul Waley (2013), Traditional retail markets: The new gentrification frontier?, p. 976. The paragraph beginning with the authors’ discussion of internal policy documents, followed by their account of tenancy clauses, photographs used to judge stalls and penalties for extending displays.

In the Kirkgate Market case, the authors describe how stall appearance was assessed through management practices. Tenancy clauses addressed displays, photographs were used to distinguish perceived strengths and challenges, and traders could be fined for extending goods or objects outside their stalls.

This passage sharpened the difference between arranging a space and deciding the conditions under which it may be arranged. A trader may put goods in place, while another actor sets the permitted boundary or the standard by which the display is judged.

It changed the questions I brought back to Rye Lane: who chooses where goods can go, who can request an adjustment, and whose agreement is needed for a larger change? The Leeds case does not establish Rye Lane’s rules. It shows why I need to identify those relationships rather than infer authority from the visible arrangement.

Produce displays and the shop edge

01 / 06

Being heard is only part of participating.

Elizabeth B.-N. Sanders and Pieter Jan Stappers (2008), Co-creation and the new landscapes of design, pp. 12–13. The discussion of people as experts in their own experience and the beginning of section 3.2 on the researcher’s changing role.

The authors distinguish a process in which information about people is passed to a designer from co-design, in which people contribute to developing knowledge, ideas and concepts. They also discuss the tools needed to support that contribution and the researcher’s role in enabling it.

This made me question what my interviews and website could claim. Including someone’s words makes their account available, but the choice of extract, image and page structure can still remain mine. Asking what they want included, showing a page and allowing them to change its content offers a more concrete opportunity to influence the representation.

This distinction also connects to the wider research question. Experience can inform an account of a place, but the ability to describe it and the ability to change its conditions are not the same. The project therefore moved towards asking who decides, who can propose an alternative, and who has the ability to make that alternative happen.
