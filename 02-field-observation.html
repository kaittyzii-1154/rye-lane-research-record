FIELD OBSERVATION

WALKING, PHOTOGRAPHING AND RETURNING TO DETAILS

The shopfront was more
than a display.

My research began with the everyday appearance and use of shopfronts on Rye Lane. Goods, signs, cardboard boxes, trolleys and temporary shelves formed a changing edge between the inside of a shop and the pavement.

Photography gave me a way to return to a detail after leaving the street. Comparing entrances, displays and the space around objects helped turn a general impression of visual density into more precise questions: where did the shop edge sit, what occupied the pavement, and what activities might the arrangement support?

Rather than treating these details as a finished facade, I began to consider the activities behind them: displaying, restocking, moving, waiting, passing through and maintaining a usable entrance. This brought the project towards the relationship between commercial space and everyday public life.

Choosing where to look more closely

Before concentrating on Rye Lane, I compared shop windows and walking routes across several London neighbourhoods. The mapping gave me a way to consider how storefronts clustered along routes and how different settings offered different kinds of visual detail. These were exploratory comparisons, based on my own observations rather than a measured ranking of neighbourhoods.

Looking across time at Holdrons Arcade

My earlier record also brought together Google Street View images of the arcade entrance. Changes to signs, colour and shutters suggested that a shopfront could be read over time, as well as during a walk. This provided a background for returning to the arcade and asking people about the place. A change in appearance alone could not establish why it happened or what it meant for those working there.
