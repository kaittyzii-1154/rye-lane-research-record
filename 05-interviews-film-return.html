INTERVIEWS, FILM & RETURN

LISTENING, EDITING AND RETURNING TO THE PEOPLE INVOLVED

From a conversation
to a continuing dialogue.

The interviews brought different relationships to Rye Lane into the project: arriving for the first time, returning to shop, working in a business, and living nearby. Questions about reasons for visiting, likes and dislikes, and desired changes connected general impressions to particular experiences.

Follow-up conversations made these accounts more concrete. A recent purchase, a conversation over coffee, or the way a record shop shares knowledge revealed aspects of the street that a photograph of its frontage could not contain.

A / Attention and visibilitySelected interview excerpts

“your eye isn't drawn anywhere in particular”

One account describes too much happening at once; another points out that people may overlook the independent businesses behind the street’s apparent chaos.

B / Routines and reasons to returnSelected interview excerpts

An account of shopping connects nearby worship, access to African food, quantity and price. A separate account recalls buying second-hand shoes for a wedding.

C / Different prioritiesSelected interview excerpts

Alongside an appreciation of independent businesses, another interview calls for a large store and easier parking to bring customers to a quieter part of the street.

D / Knowledge and exchangeRecord-shop interview excerpts

“tell people about the artist, the label”

The record-shop owner describes explaining musical connections, hosting events and bringing listeners together. Asked what the website could include, he suggests a photograph, shop name, address and video of himself talking about the shop.

E / Everyday encountersCafé interview excerpt

“we exchanged names”

The café account contrasts customers rushing for a train with those who stay to talk. A remembered exchange moves from buying coffee to discussing an evening show and introducing themselves.

F / Access and maintenanceWritten research accounts · no matching original clip identified

The CRP discusses accounts of recurring cardboard-box obstacles and waste remaining outside shops while people wait for collection. The emphasis on repetition and duration changes how these arrangements are understood.

Making the interviews into a film

The analysis helped me decide what the film needed to hold together: the density of the street, different reasons for being there, contrasting ideas of improvement, and specific moments of social exchange. These were connected themes, but not a single shared opinion.

FILM / VOICES AND TIME

Keeping encounters in sequence

The film places street impressions beside different accounts of shopping, work and social exchange. The developing edit gives more space to the record shop and café, connecting what can be seen with what people say about their everyday experience.

The editing process involved balancing the breadth of perspectives with enough time to understand a particular encounter. The short initial structure established a street question and two accounts; the expanded structure brought in more reasons for visiting and different priorities. The more compact multi-interview cut retained those differences while reducing repeated explanation.

Sound also shaped the connection between interviews and place. A voice can begin before the speaker appears and continue over street or shop footage. This lets the viewer relate an account to its setting without cutting a continuous statement into separate fragments. The shop imagery supplies context; it should not be mistaken for proof of everything being said.

RETURNING

Taking the work back
into a conversation.

As the project developed, I began to consider how the people being represented could respond to the work itself. Collecting voices was one stage of that process. Showing the website and asking what someone wanted it to communicate could allow their response to shape the content.

I also reconsidered the pace of my interviews. Moving quickly to the next question could close down an account before a person reached what mattered to them. A return conversation offered a chance to listen longer, follow a specific point and let the person guide part of the discussion.

A concrete response from the record-shop conversation

The retained interview includes a direct question about what the owner would like on the website. His response identifies a shop photograph, its name and address, and video of him introducing it. This provides a specific direction for a profile, based on what he wished to share.

This is a useful connection between listening and a design decision. It does not establish that every page was reviewed, or that the finished profile was approved. The retained material supports a contribution to the proposed content; a completed review of the website would need its own record.

What a return visit needs to make visible

The important sequence is what I showed, how the person responded, and what I changed as a result. Where a suggestion could not be implemented, that also belongs in the reflection. Recording this sequence would make it possible to explain how a return conversation influenced the work.
